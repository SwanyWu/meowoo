A Cosy Mystery

Richard Osman · The Last Devil to Die

I love a good thriller or mystery. It keeps me hooked and gives my brain something to chew on. But a good thriller also needs to earn its plot. I don’t like it when random characters or storylines are thrown in just to tie everything together, or when the ultimate explanation boils down to religion or some obscure, made-up psychological condition. It just doesn’t feel convincing enough. I guess I’m not as gullible as I was as a teenager, when I found the idea of a 33-year-old who looked like a nine-year-old completely reasonable and bought into it without question (The Orphan). These days, when the big reveal turns out to be something rooted in a cult or religion, as in The Strange House or The Building, I can’t help rolling my eyes.

The Last Devil to Die almost lost me when Elizabeth turned out to be Code 777, though I’m sure this was hinted at or explained somewhere in the previous books. I’m glad the explanation wasn’t something like “Stephen is the killer, but he has dementia” — although now I feel a little shallow for even entertaining that possibility. What I love is that everyone has a reason for doing what they do. Some are after heroin, some want the antique box, some want money, and others want power. At the end of the day, greed and curiosity go a long way towards explaining human behaviour. It’s a cosy mystery, after all, and I’m sure I would have been moved to tears when Stephen read the letter if I’d experienced something similar in real life. Anyway, it’s a perfect autumn read: things turn yellow (much like the pensioners themselves) and cosy (much like their community) at the same time. For a moment, it even makes me look forward to getting old.

Some of my favourite quotes:

“We think time travels forward, marches on in a straight line, and so we hurry alongside it to keep up. Hurry, hurry, mustn’t fall behind. But it doesn’t, you see. Time just swirls around us. Everything is always present. The things we’ve done, the people we’ve loved, the people we’ve hurt, they’re all still here.”

“We call people ‘dead’ because we need a word for it, but ‘dead’ just means that time has stopped moving forward for that person? You understand? No one dies, not really.”
